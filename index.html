Product Decisions · context before the flow

Overview

  • One-liner: a way for PMs to catch downstream dependencies before launch decisions harden.
  • Problem: downstream PMs discover breaking changes to their data too late to influence the change cheaply.
  • Solution: Automatically detect downstream data dependencies from planning artifacts and code, and give the upstream PM a one-click way to notify the downstream PM with an impact brief.
  • Value: Gives downstream teams time to adapt or push back early, reducing unplanned remediation, launch delays, and increasing product velocity.
  • North Star metric: Average number of weeks downstream PMs have before launch to act on a dependency alert, counting only alerts where they request alignment or confirm the impact.

Company type

Where this problem is most acute

  • 1,000–5,000 employees: large enough that PMs cannot be close with every downstream consumer, but not so large that data dependencies are managed by a dedicated governance team.
  • At least 10 independently owned product or data teams.
  • Product teams ship customer-facing changes at least monthly: frequent change means upstream PMs are constantly working through evolving plans, making it harder to identify and notify every affected downstream team early.
Current state

What happens now (status quo)

1

The upstream PM’s team is planning a launch that changes the data relied on by the downstream PM. The launch is 6 weeks out.

2

The upstream PM likely knows downstream dependencies may exist, but early in planning they often lack a reliable, low-cost way to know which teams are actually affected. Identifying those teams and validating the impact requires manual investigation while the proposed change is still evolving, so dependencies often remain undiscovered until implementation makes them concrete.

3

The downstream PM finds out 1 to 2 weeks before launch, when implementation or launch-readiness work exposes the dependency.

4

By then, the upstream technical approach is much harder to change, so the downstream team has little leverage to influence the design.

5

The downstream team may need to rush fixes, accept broken or inaccurate reporting, or force a late change that delays the launch.

Prototype scoping

Hypothesis: If we reduce the effort required for an upstream PM to identify a downstream data dependency and notify the downstream team about it, they will loop in downstream PMs with more lead-time to launch.

In scope

  • Detect a likely downstream dependency.
  • Show the upstream PM enough evidence to trust it.
  • Help the upstream PM notify the downstream PM early.
  • Help the downstream PM understand the potential change well enough to decide whether to initiate an alignment meeting.

Intentional gaps

  • Post-notification alignment workflow: Awareness must change behavior before replacing scheduling or resolution tools.
  • Technical feasibility assessments

What’s next

What I’d test first, ordered by risk

  1. Can the system detect real dependencies accurately? Test 3–5 past launches with known late dependencies. Maximize recall while maintaining precision above 50%.
  2. Will upstream PMs trust the evidence enough to notify? Run 5–10 moderated sessions using evidence from real planning artifacts. If they hesitate, identify whether the evidence is wrong, incomplete, or unconvincing.
  3. Will downstream PMs act on the impact brief? Show real impact briefs in moderated sessions and ask whether they would dismiss the alert or request alignment. If they still need to verify everything themselves, the brief needs richer evidence.

If the core loop validates

  • Integrate alerts into Slack, Jira, or document editors once distribution rather than trust becomes the main constraint.
  • Use confirmed and dismissed alerts plus alignment requests as training signals to improve precision and recall over time.

Product decisions

Who should initiate the alignment meeting?

Decision

The downstream PM initiates the alignment meeting, not the upstream PM.

Alternative considered

The upstream PM schedules the meeting when they click “notify,” bundling notification and meeting creation into one step.

Rationale

The upstream PM’s job is to confirm the dependency and pass it along; adding scheduling creates friction in the action we need to keep low-cost. The downstream PM knows whether the dependency requires coordination and may be able to handle it alone. Forcing a meeting on every notification wastes time and may make upstream PMs hesitate to notify.

Tradeoff

This puts coordination work on the person who did not cause the change. We accept that because the downstream PM has the stronger incentive and the context to decide whether a meeting is necessary.

Revisit signal

If downstream PMs open impact briefs but repeatedly fail to request alignment for real dependencies.

Should this be a standalone product or live inside an existing tool?

Decision

Start as a standalone product to prove the core detection and evidence experience across tools.

Alternatives considered

  • Jira or Linear feature: Easier distribution, but one platform cannot see all the signals across documents, meetings, tickets, and code.
  • Notion or Google Docs plugin: Detects risk while a PM writes, but over-optimizes for one source when evidence is spread across several.

Rationale

The biggest risk is whether PMs trust the detection and evidence enough to act. Standalone lets us test that core value before optimizing distribution.

Tradeoff and revisit signal

Standalone adds another destination for PMs. If they act on alerts but want them inside existing tools, integrations become the next distribution channel.

How should we close the awareness gap between upstream and downstream PMs?

Decision

Use event-triggered alerts with upstream PM approval before downstream notification.

Alternatives considered

  • Notify the downstream PM directly: Removes friction, but also removes the upstream PM’s judgment about whether the dependency is real. False positives could quickly erode trust. Also, an automatic data sharing approach could cause adoption issues for multiple reasons.
  • Always-on dependency dashboard (pull based): Gives full visibility, but requires PMs to remember to check it. That recreates the adoption problem caused by low perceived urgency.

Rationale

The approach is push-based, so nobody has to remember to check. The upstream PM’s one-click approval acts as a human precision filter before the downstream PM is notified, preserving alert quality without requiring manual investigation.

How should we define the North Star metric?

Decision

Average lead time of actionable downstream PM awareness: weeks between notification and launch, counted only for alerts where the downstream PM requested alignment or confirmed the impact.

Alternative considered

Average lead time of downstream PM awareness, unfiltered: the same measurement, but counting all notifications regardless of whether the downstream PM engaged.

Rationale

The unfiltered version can be inflated by firing earlier on weaker signals. If the system alerts on half-formed plans six weeks out, lead time looks great on paper, but the downstream PM cannot act because nothing is concrete enough yet. The actionable filter measures only notifications that were worth sending, combining earliness and usefulness in one number.

Tradeoff

The filtered version is harder to move because it depends on both system quality and downstream PM engagement, not just detection timing. We accept that because lowering detection thresholds should not be able to inflate the metric at the expense of real user value.

Revisit signal

If downstream PMs engage with alerts but still cannot influence the upstream design in time.

AI-assisted prototyping workflow

Tools I used

Cursor for code implementation; ChatGPT for iterating on screen UI with generated images; vanilla HTML, CSS, and JavaScript for the prototype; and GitHub Pages for deployment.

How I worked and where I steered

  • I started broad and moved progressively narrower.
  • I put more thought into major product decisions, such as problem framing, users, and metrics. The smaller the decision, the more autonomy I gave the AI tool.

Where the tool went wrong

Sometimes I asked Cursor to generate a significant amount of content, then realized it contained a product decision I should not delegate. That became a forcing function for uncovering implicit product decisions and making them deliberately.
